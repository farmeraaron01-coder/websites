## Context

I run californiafloodinsurance.com and statewidefloodinsurance.com — a California
flood insurance agency placing private (surplus lines) flood policies and NFIP
policies. We are rebuilding our "how much does flood insurance cost" page. It
currently sits at position 17.4 with 19,311 impressions over 90 days — the biggest
traffic pool on the site, stuck on page two because it answers the question
generically instead of giving real numbers.

We have real premium data from our own book. What we need from you is what we are
up against, how competitors handle one specific problem, and one statistic
verified to a citable source.

## Task 1 — Page-one teardown for the cost terms

For each of these four queries, list who ranks in the top 10 organic results
(ignore ads and Google's own AI answers):

1. `how much does flood insurance cost in california`
2. `california flood insurance cost`
3. `average cost of flood insurance california`
4. `flood insurance rates california`

For **each ranking page**, report:

- URL and site name, and what kind of site it is (carrier, aggregator/lead-gen,
  government, broker, news, comparison site)
- **Does it give an actual dollar figure, or does it hedge?** Quote the specific
  figure and the exact sentence it appears in. This is the single most important

- Where does that figure come from — do they cite FEMA, an internal study, or
  nothing at all?
- Is the price broken down by anything (flood zone, county/city, coverage amount,

- Page structure: rough word count, are there tables, is there an FAQ section, is

Format as one table plus a short note per page. Flag any result appearing in more
than one of the four queries.

## Task 2 — How does anyone handle the flood-zone price split?

This is the real question and it matters more than raw rankings.

Flood insurance pricing splits sharply by flood zone. A-zones (AE, A, AO etc.)
are high-risk and coverage is **mandatory** for anyone with a federally backed
mortgage. X-zones are lower-risk and coverage is **entirely optional** — nobody
makes you buy it. The prices are very different: an X-zone policy can be roughly
half what an A-zone policy costs.

That creates a trap. If a page publishes one blended average, the X-zone visitor —
who is a *voluntary* buyer, often prompted by El Niño coverage in the news — sees
a number roughly double what they would actually pay, and leaves. Publishing a
single average actively costs us business.

So: **across the pages you found in Task 1, plus any others you come across,
find every example of a page that prices flood insurance by flood zone.** For each:

- How do they segment — by zone letter (AE / A / X), by "high-risk vs low-risk",
  by SFHA yes/no, or some other framing?
- What figures do they show for each segment, quoted exactly?
- Do they help the reader work out which zone they are in, and how? (Map lookup,
  address tool, link to FEMA's Flood Map Service Center, a plain description?)
- Do they explain the mandatory-vs-optional distinction, and where on the page?
- Anything that reads as genuinely useful rather than filler — note it specifically

If almost nobody does this properly, say so plainly. That is a useful finding, not
a failed search.

## Task 3 — Verify one statistic, with a citable source

We want to say something like *"a large share of flood claims come from outside
high-risk flood zones"* — the point being that an X-zone property is lower risk
but not no risk, which is the honest argument for buying voluntarily.

Figures between 25% and 40% get quoted all over the internet, usually with no
source, and often as "more than 20%" or "over 40%" depending on who is selling
something.

Find **what FEMA or the NFIP actually publishes**, and give me:

- The exact figure and the exact wording
- The primary source: FEMA.gov, floodsmart.gov, an NFIP publication, or a FEMA
  press release — with a direct URL
- The date, and what period the figure covers
- Whether it counts claims, dollars paid, or policies
- If different official FEMA sources give different numbers, list each with its
  source rather than picking one

Do not give me a figure from a broker blog, an insurance aggregator, or a news
article quoting an unnamed FEMA statistic. If FEMA does not publish a clean
number, say that — we will phrase the point differently rather than cite something
we cannot stand behind.

## Ground rules

- **Quote exactly** and give URLs for everything. I will be checking.
- If you cannot verify something, say "not verified" rather than giving a

  discover is wrong after it is published.
- Do not estimate, model or infer prices. I have my own premium data for that;
