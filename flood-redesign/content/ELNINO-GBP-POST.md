## Post text

```
NOAA has an El Niño Advisory in effect — and this one is strong. As of the Climate Prediction Center's July update there's a 97% chance it lasts into spring 2027, and an 81% chance it reaches "very strong" by year end, which would rank it among the biggest since 1950.

What it means for California: El Niño reliably tilts Southern California wetter — eight of the last ten moderate-to-strong events landed in the wettest third of years. It's not a guarantee. 2015-16 was just as strong by ocean temperature and came in below average. But the odds move.

Here's what most homeowners miss. From 2014 to 2024, nearly one-third of all NFIP flood claims came from OUTSIDE high-risk flood zones. In a wet California winter the damage usually isn't a river overflowing — it's drainage that can't keep up, ground already saturated from the last storm, and runoff off wildfire burn scars. None of that follows flood-zone lines.

What it costs: in our book, a low-risk-zone California home commonly runs about $450 a year.

Your homeowners policy does not cover flood. If you're not sure whether you have it, or you only ever carried what the lender required, this is the month to check.
```

## Notes on using it

**Post it now, not in October.** The argument rests on the waiting period, which only works as a call to
action while nothing is in the forecast. By November this post argues against itself.

**The $450 is the strongest line in it.** A concrete price is what turns a weather post into an enquiry,
and it is the one thing competitors writing about El Niño will not put in print. Keep it.

**One profile, two brands.** Both sites share a single Google Business Profile under the California Flood
Insurance Services name, so this California framing is the right one — do not also post a statewide
version there.

**Update rather than repost.** NOAA refreshes ENSO on the second Thursday monthly. If the numbers move
materially, edit the figures; the specificity is what makes it credible, and stale specifics are worse
than none.

**Not in the days after a local flood.** The same text reads as opportunism. Wait a week and lead with
the claims process instead.

## The Nextdoor review — worth using, but not as-is

Eric's post corroborates the $450 well: a real low-risk-zone customer, a one-year policy at approximately
$480, bought specifically because of El Niño. That is your figure being validated in public by a customer
rather than asserted by the agency.

**Two things to sort out before it goes anywhere public.**

**Get his written OK first.** He posted to a neighbourhood feed, which is a different audience from your
website or a Google post. Republishing a named testimonial commercially goes beyond what he agreed to by
posting, and California insurance advertising rules treat testimonials as advertising content. A one-line
email — *"Eric, would you mind if we quoted your Nextdoor comment on our site?"* — settles it, and people
who write posts like that usually say yes.

**Strip the operational details either way.** The post carries a direct extension and a staff member's
first name. Neither belongs in public marketing: extensions change, and naming one person routes
strangers at a single individual and dates the material the moment they change role.

**If he says yes**, the usable form is short and keeps his own framing:

> "We just got a 1 year flood policy for approximately $480 with pretty good coverage… we have peace of
> mind for a year during the increased El Niño threat." — Eric D., Quail Lake

**If you would rather not ask**, the article already carries the substance without him — the $450 is your
own book of business, which is yours to publish and needs nobody's permission.

**One more thing worth noting from that thread.** The first reply was *"Any idea what a one year policy
would run?"* Price was the first question a real prospect asked, unprompted, before anything about
coverage or carriers. That is the argument for the cost section existing in the article at all, and for
keeping the number in the GBP post rather than making people call to get it.
