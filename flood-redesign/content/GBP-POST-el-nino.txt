===== POST TEXT — copy from the next line =====

NOAA has an El Niño Advisory in effect, and this one is strong. As of the Climate Prediction Center's July update there's a 97% chance it lasts into spring 2027 and an 81% chance it reaches "very strong" by year end — among the biggest since 1950.

For California, El Niño reliably tilts Southern California wetter: eight of the last ten moderate-to-strong events landed in the wettest third of years. It's not a guarantee. 2015-16 was just as strong and came in below average. But the odds move.

Here's what most homeowners miss. From 2014 to 2024, nearly one-third of all NFIP flood claims came from OUTSIDE high-risk flood zones. In a wet California winter the damage usually isn't a river overflowing — it's drainage that can't keep up, ground already saturated from the last storm, and runoff off wildfire burn scars. None of that follows flood-zone lines.

Cost: in our book, a low-risk-zone California home commonly runs about $450 a year.

"We just got a 1 year flood policy for approximately $480 with pretty good coverage… we have peace of mind for a year during the increased El Niño threat." — Eric D., Quail Lake

The timing trap: a new NFIP policy takes 30 days to take effect — 30 days from when you buy, not before the storm. Our private carriers can bind in as little as 7 days, commonly 10. Waiting until rain is in the forecast is waiting too long.

Your homeowners policy does not cover flood. If you're not sure whether you have it, this is the month to check.

only works as a call to action while nothing is in the forecast. By November
this post argues against itself.

read as opportunism. Wait a week and lead with the claims process instead.
