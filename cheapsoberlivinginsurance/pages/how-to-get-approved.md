## Lead

The underwriter deciding on your home will never meet you. They will not visit the house, sit in on a
morning meeting, or watch you talk somebody down at eleven at night. They will read a file.

So the work is not convincing anyone you are good at this. You already are. The work is **getting what is
already true about you onto paper**, because the file is all they get.

---

## The 40–60 word answer (place immediately after the lead, in its own block)

> Insurers writing sober living homes want evidence the operation is run as a business. In practice that
> means three things beyond the application itself: a résumé or CV showing relevant experience, membership in
> any industry association, and a simple written business plan. Homes that supply them get more offers and
> better pricing than homes that do not.

---

## §1 — Why this class gets scrutinized more than most

It is worth understanding why an underwriter asks for more here than they would for a landlord.

Claims in residential recovery are **infrequent but severe**. A slip-and-fall is a known quantity. An
allegation involving a resident's relapse, or an abuse claim, or a discharge dispute is not — and the amounts
involved can be substantial. Few carriers write this class at all, and the ones that do are careful.

That caution is not suspicion of you. It is the math of the class. But it does mean an underwriter reading
a thin file will assume the worst version of what is missing, because they have no way to check. **A thin
file gets declined or loaded, not because the operation is bad, but because the operation is unknown.**

---

## §2 — The three things that move an underwriter

Beyond the application itself, three documents do most of the work.

### A résumé or CV

**One of our carriers is specific about wanting this.** They want to see that the person running the home has
business experience, or experience in this field, or experience running any other business at all.

If you have run anything — a crew, a shop, a department, a nonprofit, a route, a trade — that belongs on the
page. If your experience is in recovery rather than in business, **that counts too, and it should be written
down as experience rather than as biography.** Years working in a treatment setting, time as a house manager
somewhere else, sponsorship, a certification, volunteer coordination — those are all relevant.

What the underwriter is asking, underneath, is: *does this person know what they are doing?* You do. The CV
is where you say so in a form they can file.

### Membership in an association

Membership in any relevant industry association signals that you are engaged in this line of work rather
than operating in isolation. It tells an underwriter you are exposed to standards, other operators, and
current practice.

State recovery-residence associations and national bodies such as NARR are the obvious examples. **Not every
good operator is a member, and membership is not a requirement** — but if you belong to something, put it in
the submission. If you do not and it is available in your state, it is usually inexpensive and it helps in
more places than insurance.

### A business plan, however simple

This is the one operators skip, and it is often the one that changes the outcome.

**A business plan does not need to be long or formal.** Two pages is plenty. What it demonstrates is that
someone is thinking about the home as a business rather than as a personal side project — and that
distinction is exactly what an underwriter is trying to establish.

A workable outline:

- **What the home is** — address, size, number of beds, owned or leased
- **Who it serves** — the population, and how residents come to you
- **Capacity and occupancy** — how many residents, typical occupancy, waiting list if any
- **Staffing** — who works there, live-in or not, employees or contractors, what each person does
- **What the home provides** — house rules, meetings, structure, transportation, testing. Be concrete
- **Money** — what residents pay, how it is collected, roughly what it costs to run the house
- **House rules and discharge** — what the rules are and what happens when one is broken
- **Emergencies** — what happens in an overdose, a fire, a medical event, an allegation
- **Where you are going** — a second house, more beds, or steady as you are

Write it plainly. Nobody is grading the prose.

## §3 — The operational facts you will be asked for anyway

Gather these before you start, and the application takes twenty minutes instead of three weeks of
back-and-forth:

- Property address, and whether the building is owned or leased
- If leased, a copy of the lease and any insurance the landlord requires
- Ownership structure — who owns the property, who operates the home, and whether they are the same entity
- Number of residents and number of bedrooms
- Years in operation
- Staffing: house managers, employees, contractors, volunteers, and whether anyone lives on site
- Services provided, honestly and in detail
- Whether residents are transported, by whom, and in whose vehicle
- Payroll figures, if you have employees
- Claims and incident history, including anything that did not become a claim
- Current insurance — the declarations pages and the exclusions, not just the carrier name
- Any licensing or certification the state requires of you

## §4 — What weakens a submission

Underwriters are not looking for perfection. They are looking for a file that hangs together.

**"We don't really do anything — we just provide housing."** This one hurts twice. It is usually inaccurate,
which leaves the operation [uninsured for what it actually does](/professional-liability/). And on an
application it reads as though the operator has not thought carefully about their own operation — which is
the opposite of the impression you want.

**Vague answers about staffing and services.** "A few people help out" invites the underwriter to imagine the
worst arrangement consistent with that sentence.

**Gaps with no explanation.** An underwriter who finds a gap will fill it with an assumption. It is always
better to state a fact plainly and briefly than to leave a hole.

**Inconsistency between documents.** If the application says six residents and the business plan says ten,
the whole file loses credibility. Read everything through once before it goes out.

**Surprises found later.** A prior claim, a lapse in coverage, an incident, a licensing issue — these are
survivable when disclosed and very damaging when discovered. Underwriters price disclosed facts. They walk
away from surprises.

---

## §5 — What to do if you don't have any of this yet

Most operators reading this do not have a CV, are not in an association and have never written a business
plan. That is normal and it is fixable in an afternoon.

1. **Write the CV first.** One page. Every job, every role, every relevant thing you have done — including
   recovery-related experience. Do not undersell time spent working in this field.
2. **Then the business plan**, using the outline in §2. Two pages. Plain sentences.
3. **Then look up your state's recovery residence association.** Join it if it is available and affordable.
4. **Then gather the documents in §3** into one folder.
5. **Then send it to us before it goes to market.** We will tell you what an underwriter is going to ask
   about, so it gets answered in the file rather than in a follow-up three weeks later.

That last step is free and it is the whole point of this page. A submission that answers questions before
they are asked gets more offers, and the offers are better. That is the most reliable way to reduce what you
pay — see [what sober living insurance costs](/sober-living-insurance-cost/).

## FAQs

**Do I really need a résumé to buy insurance?**
For this class, one carrier asks for it specifically and others weigh it. It is one page and it materially
improves your chances, so it is worth writing.

**What if my only experience is my own recovery?**
Write it down as experience. Time in this world — as a resident, a house manager, a volunteer, a sponsor — is
directly relevant to running a recovery residence, and an underwriter would rather see it stated than guess
at a blank page.

**Do I have to belong to an association?**
No. It helps, and many good operators are not members. If one is available in your state it is usually
inexpensive and useful beyond insurance.

**How long does a business plan need to be?**
Two pages is enough. The purpose is to show the home is being run as a business, not to win a competition.

**We've had a claim. Should we mention it?**
Yes. Disclose it, and say what changed afterwards. A disclosed claim with a documented response is far less
damaging than one an underwriter finds later.

**How long does approval take?**
It depends on the market and on how complete the submission is. A complete file moves in days. An incomplete
one can take weeks of back-and-forth, and sometimes never gets an offer at all.
