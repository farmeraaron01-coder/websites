## Lead

Almost every operator we talk to says a version of the same thing: *"I'm not doing anything other than
giving them a place to stay."*

It is an honest answer. It is also, in most cases, not accurate — and the evidence is usually on the
operator's own website.

---

## The 40–60 word definition (place immediately after the lead, in its own block)

> Professional liability insurance covers claims that someone was harmed by relying on your knowledge,
> advice or oversight. General liability covers physical injury and property damage on your premises.
> A sober living operator who offers structure, guidance or recovery support has both kinds of exposure,
> and one policy does not cover the other.

---

## §1 — Read your own website back to yourself

Most sober living homes market themselves honestly and effectively. The page says something close to:

- *"I've been where you are."*
- *"I know what it takes to get back on your feet."*
- *"We provide structure and accountability."*
- *"Our house rules are what make recovery stick."*

Every one of those sentences is a reason someone chose your home over the one down the street. They are
also, in insurance terms, statements that you hold particular knowledge and that residents may rely on it.

That reliance is the exposure. Not the stairs, not the water heater — the reliance.

**This is not a reason to change your marketing.** Your experience is genuinely why families trust you with
someone they love. It is a reason to insure the thing your marketing is promising.

---

## §2 — What a claim actually looks like

Operators picture liability as someone slipping on a wet floor. That is general liability, and a general
liability policy handles it.

The claims that reach a sober living operator usually look nothing like that. Illustrative, not drawn from
any specific matter:

- A resident relapses. There is an overdose, or an injury, or a death. The family's position is not that
  the property was unsafe. It is that the operator said they knew how to help, and that the house rules,
  supervision or response fell short of what was promised.
- A resident is discharged for breaking a house rule and says the decision was handled improperly, or that
  they were removed without the process the house's own materials described.
- A referral is made to a treatment provider, a sponsor or a program, and something goes wrong downstream.
- A resident's confidential information is discussed with a family member, an employer or another resident.
- An operator gives advice about medication, tapering, work, custody or legal matters — sincerely, and
  outside their training.

None of those are premises claims. Every one of them turns on judgement, advice or oversight.

**And the cost is rarely the settlement.** It is the defense. An allegation that goes nowhere still needs a
lawyer, and professional liability is often the only policy in the stack that will pay for one on a claim
of this shape.

---

## §3 — Why general liability will not respond

This is the mechanical part, and it is worth understanding once.

A general liability policy is written to cover **bodily injury and property damage** arising from your
premises and operations. It is not written to cover **economic loss caused by professional judgement**, and
most forms exclude professional services expressly.

So when a claim alleges that the harm came from what you said, decided, supervised or failed to notice —
rather than from a condition of the building — the general liability carrier's answer is usually that the
claim falls outside the form. Not out of bad faith. The policy was never priced to cover it.

That is the gap. It is not a loophole, it is a different product.

## §4 — Why it costs more, and why that is correct

Professional liability costs more than general liability for this class. We would rather explain that than
quietly leave it off the quote.

- **The claims are less predictable.** A wet floor is a known quantity. An allegation about supervision in a
  recovery setting is not.
- **Defense is expensive** even when the operator did everything right, and defense is much of what you are
  buying.
- **The market is small.** Few carriers write recovery residences at all, which is a large part of why this
  coverage is hard to find.

This site is called Cheap Sober Living Insurance because we know your margins are thin, and we will work
hard on price. Professional liability is the one line where we will argue with you about cutting it. A lower
premium that removes the coverage most likely to face a claim is not a saving; it is a deferred bill.

If another quote comes in noticeably cheaper, the useful question is not *how much* cheaper — it is *what is
different*. Ask whether professional liability is in there at all, and whether abuse and misconduct is
included or excluded. Sometimes the answer is that the other broker found a better market. Often the answer
is that something was left out.

## §5 — The part most operators do not expect

**The better you are at the mission, the more of this exposure you create.**

A landlord who collects rent and stays out of the way has very little professional liability exposure. They
also run a worse home. Every practice that makes a sober living home effective — house rules, accountability,
a manager who notices when someone is struggling, honest conversations, referrals to people who can help —
is engagement. And engagement is where this exposure comes from.

That is an uncomfortable thing to say to someone whose whole reason for opening the home was to be involved.
It is also why the coverage exists. It is not a penalty for doing the job badly. It is the cost of doing the
job at all.

Three more reasons this sits differently for a sober living operator than for most small businesses:

**The claim often comes from the family, not the resident.** A parent who paid for the placement, drove
their child to the door and was told the home would provide structure is frequently the person who calls a
lawyer. They are not evaluating your premises. They are evaluating whether the home did what it said it
would do.

**The exposure may reach you personally.** Many operators run the home as a sole proprietorship or a
single-member entity, and in plenty of cases the house itself is the business. Whether a claim can reach
personal assets depends on how the operation is structured and on facts we cannot assess from a website —
which is exactly why it is worth asking someone before a claim rather than after.

**Other people are starting to require it.** Property owners, referral partners and treatment providers
increasingly want proof of professional liability before they will lease to a home or send residents to it.
Not carrying it can quietly cost business before it ever costs a claim.

### Questions worth asking about any quote, including ours

Coverage varies by carrier, policy form and state, so these are questions rather than answers:

- Does this policy respond to allegations about guidance, supervision or decisions — or only to injuries on
  the property?
- Are defense costs paid in addition to the limit, or do they come out of it?
- Who is covered? The business, and also the operator, house managers, staff, contractors and volunteers?
- Is abuse and misconduct included here, excluded, or written as separate coverage? See
  [abuse and molestation coverage](/abuse-and-molestation-coverage/).
- If I have had coverage before, does this policy pick up incidents from those earlier years?

Ask us these. If a competing quote is cheaper, ask them the same five and compare the answers rather than
the premiums.

## §6 — Reducing the exposure, not just insuring it

Underwriters price what they can see. Operators who can show the following usually present better, and
genuinely reduce the chance of a claim:

- Written house rules, given to every resident, with a signed acknowledgement
- A documented intake process that states plainly what the home does and does not provide
- A written discharge process, followed consistently
- Incident documentation — what happened, when, what was done
- Clear boundaries in writing on medical, legal and financial advice, and referral to professionals instead
- Staff role definitions, so it is clear who is responsible for what
- Confidentiality practices covering residents' information

None of this replaces coverage. All of it makes the coverage cheaper to buy and less likely to be needed.

## FAQs

**Do I need professional liability if I just rent rooms?**
If the home only provided rooms, with no rules, structure, guidance or recovery support, the exposure would
be much smaller. In practice almost no sober living home operates that way — and most advertise the
structure as the reason to choose them. If your marketing describes experience, guidance or accountability,
assume the exposure exists.

**Isn't this covered by my general liability policy?**
Generally no. General liability covers bodily injury and property damage from your premises and operations;
most forms exclude professional services expressly. They are different products for different claims.

**What if I'm not licensed or credentialed?**
Professional liability is not limited to licensed professionals. It responds to claims that someone relied
on your knowledge or oversight. Holding yourself out as experienced is enough to create the exposure, with
or without a credential.

**Is professional liability the same as abuse and molestation coverage?**
No. They overlap in that both go beyond premises liability, but abuse and misconduct is frequently excluded
from professional liability and written as separate coverage. Both are worth reviewing.

**How much does professional liability cost for a sober living home?**
It depends on residents, staffing, services and limits — and it is typically more than general liability.
See [what sober living insurance costs](/sober-living-insurance-cost/).

**Can I add it later?**
You can. Ask, though, whether the policy would respond to something that happened before it started — many
professional liability policies do not reach backwards, so waiting can leave the time you have already been
operating uncovered. Earlier is generally better than later.
