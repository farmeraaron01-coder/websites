## Lead

If you called a general insurance agency about your home, there is a good chance you were quoted an apartment
or dwelling policy — priced on square footage, number of units and roof age. Reasonable questions for a
rental. They are the wrong questions for a house where somebody in their second week of recovery lives under
rules, with a manager, on a schedule.

Most agents are not being careless. **They have never seen one of these before.** But the policy that comes
out the other end covers a building, and what you are actually running is an operation.

## §1 — How we know: we did it ourselves

This is not a criticism we make from the outside.

**One of our first customers came to us with an apartment building.** We wrote it the way anyone would write
an apartment building — as a standard habitational risk, on the information in front of us.

Some time later we learned it was a sober living home.

Nobody had hidden anything. The owner answered the questions we asked, and we did not ask the ones that
mattered, because we did not yet know they were the ones that mattered. It had not occurred to us that a
building full of tenants could also be a program.

That is the moment this agency changed direction. We went and learned this class properly — the coverages that
get excluded, the claims that actually arrive, the handful of carriers that genuinely understand a recovery
residence and the many that say yes and mean something narrower. **We have been writing sober living homes
since 2008**, and it is a specialty rather than a line item.

**We tell that story because it is the honest version of the argument.** The agent who wrote your home as a
rental is where we were. The difference is what happens when a claim tests it.

## §2 — What a rental policy leaves out

A landlord or dwelling policy is built for a building with tenants who let themselves in and out. Written over
a recovery residence, here is what is typically missing:

**Professional liability.** General liability covers bodily injury and property damage. It excludes
professional services. Every house meeting, house rule, referral and piece of guidance sits in that
excluded space — and that is where the serious claims in this class come from. See
[professional liability](/professional-liability/).

**Abuse and molestation.** Commonly excluded from general liability, and frequently the single largest
exposure a home carries. See [abuse and molestation coverage](/abuse-and-molestation-coverage/).

**Anything to do with staff.** A house manager is an exposure a landlord does not have — workers'
compensation, employment claims, and the supervision questions that come with authority over residents.

**Driving residents.** Meetings, appointments, work, court. Personal auto excludes business use, and most
homes are using somebody's own car. See
[commercial auto and transporting residents](/commercial-auto-and-transporting-residents/).

**Business activity at all.** Many dwelling and homeowners forms exclude business use of the premises and
limit unrelated occupants. That exclusion can reach further than people expect. See
[is my current policy enough?](/is-my-current-policy-enough/)

**Loss of income that reflects how the home earns.** Rent loss on a landlord form is not the same thing as
a home that has to keep paying a manager while it cannot take residents.

## §3 — The quieter problem: the policy describes the wrong property

This is the part that concerns us more than any single missing coverage.

Insurance is priced on what the carrier was told. If the application and the policy describe an apartment
building, and the property is in fact a recovery residence with a manager, house rules and structured
requirements, that is not a small discrepancy — **it is a different risk than the one the carrier agreed to
take.**

Where a risk has been materially misdescribed, a carrier can dispute the claim or move to rescind the policy.
Whether it happens turns on the specific facts and on state law, so nobody should promise you either outcome.
But the exposure is real, and the moment it surfaces is the worst possible moment: after a loss, with a lawyer
already involved.

**Almost none of this is the operator's fault.** You answered the questions you were asked. That is exactly
why the questions matter, and it is why we ask about staffing and services before we ask about the roof.

If you are not certain how your current policy describes your property, send us the declarations page. Reading
it costs nothing and takes us a few minutes.

## §4 — Why so few agents know this class

Worth saying plainly, because it is structural rather than a knock on anyone:

- **Most agents will see a handful of these in an entire career.** There is no way to build judgment on a risk
  you meet once every few years.
- **Very few carriers write it.** An agent without those appointments cannot place it properly even after
  recognizing what it is — so it gets written on the form they do have.
- **It does not look unusual on the surface.** It is a house, with people living in it, paying to be there.
  The thing that makes it different is invisible from the street and absent from a standard application.
- **The vocabulary hides it.** Sober living home, recovery residence, transitional housing, structured living.
  An agent who has not worked in this field does not necessarily hear "program" in any of those.

There are not many markets for this class. That is the actual reason a specialist matters — not a secret list,
but knowing which carriers understand a recovery residence, and what a submission has to say to get a real
answer from them.

## §5 — What we do differently

- **We ask about the operation before the building.** Who works there, who lives on site, what the house
  requires of residents, whether anyone drives them, how a discharge is handled. Those answers determine the
  coverage; square footage determines the price.
- **We read your current policy, including the exclusions.** Not the carrier name — the actual form. Most

## §6 — Why "my agent says he can do it" is not enough

Any agent can tell you they write sober living homes. Saying it costs nothing, and most of the people who say
it believe it.

Here is the part that rarely gets said out loud: **if it is written wrong, you are the one holding the claim.**

Not the agent. A demand arrives naming the home, and the home has to answer it now — with a lawyer, on a
deadline. If the policy turns out not to respond, going after the agent afterward is a second, separate
lawsuit: slow, expensive, uncertain, and it does nothing about the claim already in front of you. You would
be funding two fights at once, one of them while uninsured.

And that second fight is not a guaranteed recovery. **Agents are not required everywhere to carry coverage for
their own mistakes**, the limits vary enormously among those who do, and a limit chosen for routine errors may
sit well below what a serious claim in this class produces. You would find out which after the fact.

**Weigh that against what is actually at stake.** The heaviest exposures in a recovery residence involve a
resident being seriously harmed or dying. Claims of that shape are not five-figure events, and no operating
budget absorbs one. Set against that, the premium difference between a policy that answers and a policy that
does not is small — and it is the only part of this you get to decide in advance.

None of that is a reason to distrust your agent. It is a reason not to rely on **"I can handle that"** as your
only diligence. Ask instead:

1. **Which carriers do you place recovery residences with, and roughly how many have you written?** A
   specialist answers immediately. There is no wrong answer, only a vague one.
2. **On what you are quoting me, is abuse and molestation included or excluded — and is defense inside the
   limit or outside it?** Anyone who works in this class knows this without looking.
3. **What is your professional liability limit?** A fair question, and any professional should be able to
   answer it about their own business.

Ask us the same three. We will answer all of them.

## FAQs

**My current agent has handled my insurance for years. Are you saying they did something wrong?**
No. In almost every case they were never told the property was a recovery residence, or were told and had no
market that writes one. It is worth having the policy read by someone who knows this class, which is free and
does not require changing anything.

**Can't any agent just add the coverages I'm missing?**
Only if they have carriers that write them for this class, and most do not. Abuse and molestation in
particular is not something you add to a landlord policy.

**Is a sober living home really that different from an apartment building?**
Yes, in the one way insurance cares about. An apartment building houses tenants. A recovery residence houses
residents and provides structure, and providing structure is a professional exposure. That difference is the
whole subject.

**What if my policy already says it's a sober living home?**
Then you are ahead of most, and the next question is whether the coverages match the operation — professional
liability, abuse and molestation, staff, and auto. Send the declarations page and we will tell you.

**If my agent gets it wrong, can't I just hold them responsible?**
Sometimes, but it is a second lawsuit rather than a remedy. The claim against your home still has to be
defended now, on its own deadline, and pursuing your agent runs separately and slowly afterward. Agents are
also not required everywhere to carry coverage for their own errors, and limits vary widely. It is not a plan
you would choose in advance.

**Isn't this just an argument for paying more?**
No — this site exists partly because operators are overcharged, and we compete on price. The argument is
narrower: never buy the *wrong* policy because it is cheaper. Paying less for coverage that answers is the
goal. Paying less for coverage that does not answer is not a saving, it is an uninsured loss with a receipt.

**How long have you specialized in this?**
We have been writing sober living homes since 2008. It started by getting one wrong — see §1.
