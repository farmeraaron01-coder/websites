## Lead

This is the hardest page on this site to write, and probably the hardest one to read.

Nobody opens a sober living home expecting to think about this. But a home full of adults in early
recovery — many carrying trauma, living in shared space, sometimes with staff who have keys and authority —
is a setting where this exposure exists. Insurers know it. Plaintiffs' lawyers know it. Most operators find
out when they read an exclusion.

**Two things are true at once.** Preventing harm is the point, and no policy substitutes for that. And a home
that does everything right can still face an allegation, and needs to survive it.

## The 40–60 word definition (place immediately after the lead, in its own block)

> Abuse and molestation coverage responds to claims alleging sexual misconduct, abuse or molestation
> connected to your operation — including the cost of defending the claim. General liability and
> professional liability policies frequently exclude it. For a sober living home it is often available as
> separate coverage, and for many operations it is the single largest exposure they carry.

---

## §1 — Why this exposure is different in a recovery residence

Generic guidance treats this as a staff-to-client risk. In a sober living home the picture is wider, and
this is the part most operators have not been walked through:

**Staff and residents.** House managers and staff hold authority, keys and access. That relationship is
inherently unequal, which is exactly why claims in this category are taken so seriously.

**Resident to resident.** This is the exposure operators least expect. If one resident harms another, the
claim against the home is usually not that the operator did it — it is that the home failed in supervision,
in room or house assignment, in how it responded to an earlier report, or in the rules it set and did not
enforce. The home can face a claim without anyone on staff doing anything wrong.

**Volunteers, contractors and visitors.** People who are not employees still come and go: maintenance, peer
volunteers, alumni, family. Whether a policy treats them as covered persons varies, and it is worth asking.

**Reporting and response.** How a home responds to a disclosure is frequently what a claim turns on. Not
only whether something happened, but what happened next.

None of this is a reason to run the home defensively or to treat residents as risks. It is the reason the
coverage exists and the reason underwriters ask about house procedures.

---

## §2 — Check whether you already have it. You probably do not.

**Abuse and molestation is commonly excluded from general liability**, and often from professional liability
as well. It is frequently written as its own coverage or added by endorsement, and for sober living homes it
is available as standalone coverage.

That means three things worth doing before anything else:

1. **Look for an exclusion.** On your current policy, look for wording about abuse, molestation, sexual
   misconduct or sexual abuse. If you find it in the exclusions, you are not covered for it no matter how
   large your liability limit is.
2. **A large general liability limit does not help.** Operators sometimes assume a $1M or $2M limit covers
   everything. An exclusion removes the entire category, not part of it.
3. **Ask whether defense is included.** For this category the defense is often the largest cost, and
   whether it is covered — and whether it is paid alongside the limit or out of it — is worth asking about
   any quote.

If you are not sure what your policy says, send us the declarations page and the exclusions. Reading it is
free.

## §3 — What drives the cost

Premium for this coverage is priced on a small number of specific things:

| Factor | What underwriters are looking at |
|---|---|
| **Size and scope of the operation** | Number of residents, number of homes, how many employees have contact with residents. More people in contact means more exposure |
| **The nature of the population served** | Homes serving people who are more vulnerable — early recovery, co-occurring conditions, court-involved residents, young adults — are assessed differently. This is about exposure, not about the worth of the people served |
| **Claims and incident history** | Prior claims, and also how prior incidents were documented and handled |
| **Screening and risk management** | Hiring practices, training, house rules, supervision, and how reports are handled. **This is the factor you control** — see below |
| **Limits and structure** | The limit chosen, and whether the coverage sits inside another policy or stands alone |

We will not publish premium numbers here that we cannot stand behind. Ask and we will tell you what your
operation is likely to see.

## §4 — The one factor you control, and the part generic advice gets wrong

Every insurance article on this subject says "screen your employees." For a sober living home that advice is
incomplete to the point of being useless, because **many of the best house managers in this industry are
people in recovery, and some of them have records.**

Hiring people with lived experience is not a weakness in this model. It is frequently the reason the home
works. So the useful version of this advice is not *exclude anyone with a history* — it is:

- **Have a written screening process, and apply it the same way every time.** Consistency is what an
  underwriter and a court both look for. An inconsistent process is worse than a modest one.
- **Decide in advance what is disqualifying and what is not, and write down why.** A documented,
  reasoned standard is defensible. An ad-hoc decision is not.
- **Take advice on what you may lawfully ask and consider.** Fair-chance hiring rules vary by state and
  locality, and this is a question for an employment attorney rather than for an insurance broker or a
  website.
- **Train on boundaries explicitly**, and document that you did. Two-person rules where practical, clear
  policy on being alone with a resident, on physical contact, on relationships between staff and residents.
- **Write down the reporting path** — who a resident tells, what happens next, who is notified, what gets
  recorded — and make sure every resident knows it on day one.
- **Document incidents contemporaneously.** What was reported, when, what was done. Notes made at the time
  are worth far more than a recollection two years later.
- **Think about supervision and layout** — sightlines, room assignments, overnight coverage, who has keys.

Every item on that list reduces the chance of harm to a resident. That is the reason to do it. That it also
presents better to an underwriter, and may reduce what you pay, is a genuine second benefit — not the
motive.

## §5 — What the coverage actually does for the home

- **It pays to defend the allegation.** Even a claim that goes nowhere requires a lawyer, and in this
  category that cost arrives quickly.
- **It keeps a claim from ending the operation.** For most homes there is no reserve that absorbs a
  defended abuse claim. Without coverage the realistic outcome is that the home closes, and the residents
  living there lose their housing.
- **It satisfies people who require it.** Property owners, referral partners, treatment providers and some
  certifying bodies increasingly want to see this coverage before they will lease to a home or send
  residents to it.

Coverage terms, limits and what counts as a covered person vary by carrier and policy. These are the right
questions to ask, not a description of any particular policy.

## FAQs

**Isn't this already in my general liability policy?**
Usually not. Abuse and molestation is commonly excluded from general liability and often from professional
liability too. Check the exclusions on your current policy — a large liability limit does not help if the
category is excluded.

**Can I buy it on its own?**
Yes. For sober living homes this is available as standalone coverage as well as, in some cases, an
endorsement to another policy.

**What if the allegation is between two residents and my staff did nothing wrong?**
That is one of the more common shapes a claim takes, and it is why the coverage matters. The claim against
the home usually alleges a failure in supervision, screening, house rules or response rather than direct
wrongdoing by the operator.

**Will buying this coverage make it look like I expect something to happen?**
No. Underwriters, property owners and referral partners read it the opposite way — as a sign the operation
is run seriously. Not carrying it is what stands out.

**We have never had an incident. Do we still need it?**
Most homes that face a claim had never had one before. Premium is priced partly on claims history, so a
clean record helps what you pay — it does not remove the exposure.

**Does hiring people in recovery raise my premium?**
It is not the fact of lived experience that underwriters price; it is whether you have a documented,
consistently applied screening and training process. See §4.
