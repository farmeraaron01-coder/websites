Texas has more flood insurance policies in force than any state except Florida, and the pricing in our book shows why the market is so large: most Texas flood insurance is sold outside the high-risk floodplain. Across 367 Texas properties we quoted between February 2025 and August 2026, the median all-in private premium was $670 a year — but that single number hides a $278 gap between zones.

What flood insurance costs in Texas

Median all-in cost is $670 a year. The middle half of quotes fall between $509 and $970, the cheapest tenth under $469, and the most expensive tenth above $1,272. “All-in” means the premium plus policy fee and Texas surplus lines taxes — the figure on the bill.

Most Texas flood insurance is voluntary

Fifty-eight percent of the Texas properties we quote sit in Zone X — outside the mapped high-risk floodplain, where no federal rule requires cover and no lender is obliged to ask for it. These are people buying flood insurance because they decided to, not because they were told to.

That is a very different market from a state like Arizona or Oklahoma, where high-risk zones dominate the book. It is also, in our view, the correct read of Texas flood risk. Harvey in 2017 flooded tens of thousands of homes outside the 100-year floodplain; the map is a regulatory boundary, not a promise. FEMA reports that from 2014 to 2024, 29% of NFIP claims came from areas outside high-risk flood zones (FEMA, FloodSmart.gov).

The $278 zone gap

Texas Zone AE prices at $892 against Zone X at $614 — a gap of $278 a year, and one of the widest zone spreads in our national book. Texas AE is also among the most expensive AE anywhere we write, comparable to Florida and well above California’s $763.

The reason is depth. Texas coastal and bayou AE zones carry genuine surge and deep-riverine exposure, and under Risk Rating 2.0 that gets priced on the building’s actual vulnerability rather than averaged across the zone. Where the hazard is real, the price reflects it. Where it is shallow or infrequent, it does not.

Where in Texas we quote

Houston is our single largest Texas market by a wide margin, followed by the surrounding suburbs — Cypress, Spring, Katy and Pearland. That clustering is not an accident of our marketing; it is where the demand is. Harris County and its neighbours combine flat terrain, heavy clay soils, rapid development and extreme rainfall, and the result is a metropolitan area where properties well outside the floodplain still flood.

What drives your Texas premium

Nine in ten Texas quotes carry a $5,000 deductible, and the median building limit is $250,000 — the NFIP’s statutory ceiling. About 16% of Texas properties we quote need more than $250,000 of building coverage, which the federal programme cannot provide at any price. For those homes the private market is not a cheaper alternative; it is the only route to being fully covered.

Nearly nine in ten Texas properties we price are primary residences. Beyond that the rating model looks at first-floor height, distance to water, foundation type, replacement cost and prior claims.

Frequently asked questions

Is flood insurance required in Texas?
Only with a federally backed mortgage on a property in a high-risk zone — A, AE, AO or AH. Texas has no state requirement. But most Texas policies we write are on properties where nobody required it.

Why is Texas Zone AE so expensive?
Depth and surge. Coastal and bayou AE zones in Texas carry deeper, faster water than the national average, and Risk Rating 2.0 prices the building’s real exposure rather than averaging across a zone label. Our Texas AE median is $892 against a $614 Zone X median.

Does homeowners insurance cover flooding in Texas?
No. Every standard homeowners policy excludes flood. Wind-driven rain through a storm-damaged roof is a separate peril that may be covered, which is why hurricane claims often involve both policies and why the distinction matters so much on the Gulf Coast.

Do I need flood insurance in Houston if I am not in a flood zone?
Everywhere is in a flood zone; the question is which one. Zone X means lower risk, not no risk, and the Houston metro has repeatedly flooded homes outside the mapped floodplain. At a $614 median it is an inexpensive decision to get wrong in the other direction.
