Florida has more flood insurance policies in force than any other state, and it is the state where the federal programme most often runs out of room. Across 342 Florida properties we quoted between February 2025 and August 2026, the median all-in private premium was $681 a year — but one Florida property in five needs more building coverage than the NFIP is legally allowed to write. That is the highest share of any state in our book.

What flood insurance costs in Florida

Median all-in cost is $681 a year. The middle half of quotes fall between $492 and $984. The tails are the widest we see anywhere: the cheapest tenth come in under $472, and the most expensive tenth run past $1,643. “All-in” means premium plus policy fee and Florida surplus lines taxes — the number on the bill.

Where the federal programme runs out

The NFIP’s residential building limit is $250,000. It is statutory — Congress sets it, and no underwriter can raise it. Twenty-one percent of the Florida properties we quote carry building limits above that ceiling, against 16% in Texas and 9% in Arizona.

For those homes a federal policy is not merely more expensive or less generous; it is structurally incapable of covering the building. A $500,000 Florida home with an NFIP policy is insured for half its rebuild cost, and no amount of shopping the federal programme changes that. Private markets write well above the cap, and for that fifth of the Florida market it is the only route to being made whole.

The widest spread in the country

Florida’s tenth-to-ninetieth percentile range runs from $472 to $1,643 — a 3.5× spread, and the widest of any state we write. A state median is a genuinely poor guide to your own address here.

That range is geography. Florida contains inland Zone X neighbourhoods with modest, shallow rainfall exposure and coastal AE properties facing storm surge, sitting a few miles apart. Zone AE at $895 is the most expensive AE median in our national book — above Texas at $892, and well above California at $763 — because Florida surge is genuinely deeper and more frequent than most of what the model prices elsewhere.

Most Florida buyers are outside the floodplain

Fifty-eight percent of the Florida properties we quote sit in Zone X. As in Texas, most private flood insurance here is bought voluntarily rather than because a lender demanded it — which fits a state where FEMA reports 29% of NFIP claims nationally come from outside high-risk flood areas (FEMA, FloodSmart.gov), and where hurricane rainfall routinely floods homes the maps call low risk.

Where in Florida we quote

Our Florida book is unusually evenly spread — Naples, Pensacola, Miramar, Vero Beach and Edgewater lead, with no single metro dominating. That is different from Texas, where Houston and its suburbs account for a large share, and it reflects the simple fact that Florida’s flood exposure is statewide rather than concentrated in one basin.

What drives your Florida premium

Nine in ten Florida quotes carry a $5,000 deductible. Around nine in ten properties we price are primary residences. Beyond coverage amount, the model prices first-floor height above grade, distance to the coast or nearest waterway, foundation and construction type, replacement cost, and prior claims. Elevation certificates matter more in Florida than almost anywhere, because a foot of first-floor height changes surge exposure materially.

Frequently asked questions

Is flood insurance required in Florida?
Only with a federally backed mortgage on a property in a high-risk zone. There is no statewide requirement — though many Florida lenders, condo associations and HOAs impose their own, which is a separate obligation from the federal rule.

Can I get more than $250,000 of flood coverage in Florida?
Not from the NFIP; that limit is set by Congress. Private markets write well above it, and about a fifth of the Florida properties we quote need them to.

Why is Florida Zone AE the most expensive in the country?
Storm surge. Florida AE zones carry deeper, more frequent coastal flooding than AE zones inland or on the Pacific, and Risk Rating 2.0 prices that real exposure rather than averaging it across the zone label.

Does homeowners insurance cover hurricane flooding?
No. Wind damage is covered by your homeowners or windstorm policy; rising water is not, and never is. Hurricane claims frequently involve both perils, and which policy pays turns on how the water reached the building — which is why documenting damage carefully matters.
